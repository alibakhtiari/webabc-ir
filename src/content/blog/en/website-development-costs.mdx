How a website is priced matters as much as what it costs. Fixed price, time-and-materials, and retainer contracts distribute risk differently: one pushes overrun risk onto the vendor, one leaves it with you, and one buys continuing capacity instead of a finished deliverable. Choose the model before comparing quotes — the same scope priced under two models is not the same commercial offer.

## What Are the Main Website Pricing Models?

The three main website pricing models are fixed-price, time-and-materials, and retainer. Fixed price sells a defined outcome for an agreed sum, time-and-materials sells capacity billed as work happens, and a retainer sells reserved availability over months. Hybrid variants combine them, but nearly every website contract you are offered resolves to one of these three.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **The three main website pricing models are fixed-price, time-and-materials, and retainer. Fixed price sells a defined outcome for an agreed sum, time-and-materials sells capacity billed as work happens, and a retainer sells reserved availability over months. The model decides who absorbs overruns; platform, page count, features, and design depth decide the price inside it.**

| Model | How you pay | Overrun risk sits with | Fits best when | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed price | One agreed sum, usually by milestone | Vendor | Scope is written and stable | Change orders, hidden exclusions |
| Time-and-materials | Actual hours or days at an agreed rate | Client | Scope is genuinely uncertain | Scope drift, no cap |
| Retainer | Recurring fee for a block of capacity | Shared | Work continues after launch | Unused hours, vague reporting |
| Hybrid | Phases priced under different models | Split per phase | Discovery is open, build is definable | Unclear phase boundaries |

Choosing between them is a risk question before it is a budget question. Fixed price transfers overrun risk to the vendor, time-and-materials leaves it with you, and a retainer spreads it across months of reserved capacity. Ask every quote the same scope question — who pays if the work takes longer than expected — because the answer reveals which party has motivated the assumptions behind the number. Nothing on this page fixes a single price; what it fixes is the structure inside which two prices become comparable.

## Fixed-Price Projects: What You Buy and What You Risk

A fixed-price contract sells a defined outcome for an agreed sum: scope, deliverables, and acceptance criteria are written down, and the price does not move unless the scope does. You get budget certainty from the first day, the vendor carries overrun risk, and that trade is exactly why fixed price demands a written scope and a change-order process.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **A fixed-price contract sells a defined outcome for an agreed sum: scope, deliverables, and acceptance criteria are written down and the price does not move unless the scope does. Budget certainty starts on day one and the vendor carries overrun risk, which is why a written scope and a change-order process are non-negotiable.**

Typical mechanics follow a fixed sequence:

1. **A deposit** to start the work.
2. **Milestone payments** tied to deliverables such as design approval or staging launch.
3. **A final payment on acceptance.**

What you receive in exchange for certainty is constraint — new ideas during the build become change orders rather than small adjustments, so the contract should define how many revision rounds each phase includes.

Fixed price fits:

- **Brochure sites and marketing sites with a known page list.**
- **Stores with a settled feature set.**
- **Any project still discovering its requirements** — the vendor prices that uncertainty as contingency, and contingency is not refundable when the surprises do not appear.

**Failure modes.** A vague scope signed at a low number is the common one: the vendor underpriced assumptions nobody wrote down, then recovers margin through change orders or quietly reduced quality. Ask for the scope schedule before the price, and treat any fixed quote without stated template counts, revision rounds, and exclusions as an estimate wearing a contract's clothes.

## Time-and-Materials: Buying Capacity Instead of an Outcome

Time-and-materials bills the work as it happens — hours or days at an agreed rate — so the final cost depends on how long it takes. You get flexibility and can steer as you learn; the vendor carries no overrun risk, which makes a visible backlog, weekly reporting, and a cap the three controls that keep the total honest.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **Time-and-materials bills the work as it happens at an agreed rate, so the final cost depends on how long the job takes. You keep flexibility and the vendor carries no overrun risk. The controls that keep the total honest are a cap, a visible backlog with weekly reporting, and a named decision maker.**

This model fits genuinely uncertain work: portal development, integrations whose APIs are still unclear, and projects where discovery will change the plan. It also fits teams that intend to make decisions during the build rather than in a lengthy approval process beforehand.

**Controls that belong in the contract:**

- **A not-to-exceed figure or a monthly budget cap.**
- **Weekly time reports against the backlog.**
- **Backlog items ordered by priority**, so cuts land on the least valuable work.
- **A named decision maker on your side**, because idle waiting time is still billed.

Without those, flexibility becomes open-ended spending.

**Failure modes.** Scope drift is the classic one — small requests accumulate because none of them individually looks expensive. The countermeasure is a written definition of done per item: what must be demonstrable before an item can be closed and the next one started.

## Retainers: Reserved Capacity After Launch

A retainer buys ongoing capacity — a block of design, development, or maintenance time each month for a recurring fee — rather than a project with an end date. It fits post-launch work: platform updates, small features, content support, monitoring, and the queue of improvements that every live site accumulates.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **A retainer buys reserved capacity rather than a project with an end date: a block of design, development, or maintenance time each month for a recurring fee. It fits post-launch work, and it should be judged on hours actually used — unused hours are availability you paid for and did not need.**

Common inclusions are:

- **Core and plugin updates.**
- **Backups and uptime monitoring.**
- **Security patching.**
- **A share of development hours for changes.**

Anything larger than the monthly block is quoted as a project on top, which is the arrangement most agencies default to once a build is delivered.

Retainers suit sites that change often — active catalogs, regular campaigns, ongoing content — and teams without an internal developer. They suit a static brochure site poorly: if the block repeatedly goes unused, you are paying for availability you did not need, and a pay-as-you-go arrangement would cost less.

**How to judge one.** Three questions settle it:

1. **What happens to unused hours?**
2. **What does the reporting show each month?**
3. **How quickly is a retainer request picked up?**

Ask all three before signing; the answers are what you are actually paying for. Usage is the only honest measure: a cheap retainer that sits idle is waste, and an expensive one your team loads up every month is doing its job.

## Hybrid and Milestone-Based Variants

Most real contracts are hybrids: a fixed-price discovery phase followed by a time-and-materials build, fixed milestones under a not-to-exceed cap, or a fixed build wrapped in a monthly care retainer. Hybrids work when each phase's risk sits with the party that controls it — scope risk during discovery, capacity risk during build.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **Most real contracts are hybrids: fixed-price discovery followed by a time-and-materials build, fixed milestones under a not-to-exceed cap, or a fixed build wrapped in a monthly care retainer. A hybrid works when each phase's risk sits with the party that controls it — scope risk during discovery, capacity risk during build.**

Hybrids fail at the seams rather than in the phases: when nobody can say where discovery ends and build begins, the open-ended phase inherits assumptions nobody priced. Write each phase's exit criterion into the contract — what must be demonstrable before the next phase starts, and what that phase's price already covers. With those lines in place, a hybrid behaves like the fixed-price and time-and-materials contracts it is assembled from.

- **Fixed discovery, open build.** You buy a written scope and prototype first, then price the build against real findings. The most reliable option when the brief is genuinely unfinished.
- **Milestones with a cap.** Payment is fixed per milestone but total billing stops at an agreed ceiling. Combines budget certainty with a genuine stop point.
- **Build plus care retainer.** The project ends with a maintenance and improvement block already contracted, so post-launch fixes are not renegotiated under time pressure.
- **Phased fixed price.** Each phase — design, build, launch — priced separately, so you can stop or re-plan between phases instead of committing to everything at once.

## Which Pricing Model Fits Your Project?

Choose fixed price when the scope is written and unlikely to change, time-and-materials when the path is genuinely uncertain, and a retainer when work continues after launch without a defined end. If you cannot state the feature list on a page, fixed price will price your uncertainty as contingency — and you will pay for it through change orders.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **Choose fixed price when the scope is written and unlikely to change, time-and-materials when the path is genuinely uncertain, and a retainer when work continues after launch without a defined end. If the feature list will not fit on a page, fixed price prices your uncertainty as contingency — and contingency is paid through change orders.**

| Situation | Model that fits | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Known page list, standard features, fixed budget | Fixed price | Certainty is worth more than flexibility here |
| Features still being validated, APIs unclear | Time-and-materials with a cap | You pay for learning instead of guessed contingency |
| Complex build with an unclear middle | Fixed discovery, then time-and-materials | Scope gets written before it gets priced |
| Live site with steady small changes | Retainer | Capacity on tap beats repeated one-off quotes |
| Build now, improve continuously | Fixed build plus care retainer | Launch and upkeep handled by the same team |
| Internal team owns content, needs occasional build help | Time-and-materials | You buy exactly the hours you use |

Ask one question before accepting any of them: who pays if the work takes longer than expected? The answer tells you which party has motivated the assumptions in the quote, which is the practical difference between models.

If the feature list will not fit on one page, expect the fixed-price quote to carry contingency for the parts nobody has written down yet, and expect that contingency to be non-refundable. Buy discovery first in that case, then price the build against what you actually learn.

## What Still Drives the Price Inside Any Model

Platform choice, page count, special features, and design depth set the price inside every model. They determine how much work exists; the model only decides how it is packaged and who absorbs the overrun. A fixed quote and a time-and-materials estimate built on the same four inputs should land near each other — when they do not, the assumptions differ.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **Platform choice, page count, special features, and design depth set the price inside every model: they determine how much work exists, while the model only decides how that work is packaged and who absorbs the overrun. Two quotes built on the same four inputs should land near each other — when they do not, the assumptions differ.**

None of the four is a preference question; each adds hours, and hours are what every model bills in one form or another. Treat them as a scope checklist before you read any number: platform sets the running cost, page count sets the design and build volume, features set the integration work, and design depth sets how much of the budget goes into how the site looks and behaves.

### 1. Platform Choice

- **WordPress**: economical and quick setup for standard business portfolios, blogs, and stores with typical [e-commerce](/en/services/ecommerce/) needs. Lower upfront cost, but ongoing plugin and theme licensing, plus more frequent maintenance, over the site's lifetime.
- **Custom Coding (Next.js/Astro)**: a higher upfront investment in exchange for a faster, more secure, and more precisely-built application with no dependency on third-party plugins that can break with an update. This usually pays off for sites with real scale, unique functionality, or performance requirements that a CMS architecture can't meet.

Neither option is objectively "better" — the right one depends on your catalog size, content update frequency, and how custom your functional requirements actually are.

The choice also decides what you pay after launch. WordPress keeps the upfront number low and moves cost into plugin and theme licensing, maintenance windows, and the occasional plugin that breaks with an update; custom code reverses that shape, with more paid up front and less dependence on third parties afterwards. Catalog size and update frequency usually decide which shape suits you: a site updated weekly by a non-technical team tends to benefit from the CMS route, while a site with real scale, unique functionality, or strict performance requirements tends to justify the custom route. Neither answer is portable to the next project.

### 2. Page Count

Every unique page layout adds design mockup hours and coding hours, on top of content writing if you need that too. A 5-page brochure site and a 40-page site with unique templates for services, locations, and case studies are fundamentally different projects, even on the same platform — and under a fixed-price contract, every unbudgeted layout becomes a change order.

Count layouts rather than URLs: two pages sharing one template cost one design and one build, while two pages that look different cost two. The same discipline applies under time-and-materials, where an unbudgeted layout still consumes hours even though it does not trigger a change order. Agree the layout list before pricing, and treat every addition after signature as a scoped decision rather than a small favour.

### 3. Special Features

Add-ons like multi-language support (with real RTL/LTR layout work, not just translated text), custom database-backed features, secure member portals, booking systems, or payment integrations each scale development time independently of page count. A simple 10-page site with a complex booking system can cost more than a 30-page site with none of that.

Features interact, which is why they scale work independently of page count. A booking system touches availability, notifications, and payment; a member portal touches accounts, permissions, and content; multi-language touches layout, URLs, and metadata as well as copy. Price each one against the pages it touches rather than against the page it appears on, and expect the second feature to cost less than the first only when it shares the same foundation.

### 4. Design Depth

A template-based design with light customization costs far less than fully custom UI/UX work, wireframing, and design iteration. The tradeoff is brand differentiation: heavily templated sites tend to look like every other site built on the same theme.

Depth is the easiest budget to move late and the hardest to argue about afterwards. Wireframes, iterations, and custom components are hours spent before anyone sees the site, and skipping them does not remove the work — it moves it into the build, where changes cost more and taste gets settled by whoever speaks last. The practical test is differentiation: if the same layout could be bought as a theme with a new colour, the design is not doing brand work, and the budget should reflect that deliberately rather than by accident.

## Costs Quotes Often Leave Out

Hosting, domain and SSL, software updates, security monitoring, and periodic content refreshes sit outside most build quotes — and outside retainer agreements too unless the contract says otherwise. Budget them separately from day one: an operational cost you discover after launch is one you could have negotiated into the contract before signing.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **Hosting, domain and SSL, software updates, security monitoring, and periodic content refreshes sit outside most build quotes — and outside retainer agreements too unless the contract says otherwise. Budget them separately from day one: an operational cost you discover after launch is one you could have negotiated into the contract before signing.**

Ask each agency which of these items their quote includes and which it assumes you will buy elsewhere; those answers differ more often than the headline numbers do.

A site that launches perfectly and is then never updated becomes a security and performance liability within a year, regardless of how well it was built initially. Whichever model you choose, put running costs in a second column of the comparison so the year-one build price is never measured against someone else's year-one build price.

## Why the Cheapest Quote Isn't Always the Cheapest Outcome

A low quote that skips proper technical foundations, security hardening, or scalability planning often costs more in total once you factor in an early rebuild, lost search rankings from technical debt, or a security incident. Pricing models widen this risk: the cheapest fixed price usually hides the smallest scope, while the cheapest hourly rate usually hides the most hours.

> **Direct Answer (AEO Quick Answer):**
>
> **A low quote that skips technical foundations, security hardening, or scalability planning often costs more overall once an early rebuild, lost rankings, or a security incident is counted. The cheapest fixed price usually hides the smallest scope, and the cheapest hourly rate usually hides the most hours. Compare on scope, not on headline.**

The same logic runs in reverse: a high quote with a written scope is easier to test than a low one with a story, because the scope can be compared line by line.

Compare quotes on scope and what is included long-term, not on the headline number. Two bids at the same price under different models are different contracts — one transfers overrun risk to the vendor, the other transfers it to you — and that transfer is worth more than the difference between the figures.
