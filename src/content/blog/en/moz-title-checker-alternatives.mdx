## What does a title checker actually have to measure?

A useful title checker answers five separate questions, and most free tools answer only two of them. Will the draft fit the character band? Will it fit Google's pixel budget in the font Google actually renders? Does it survive a phone screen? Is the published title the one you wrote? Does the resulting snippet earn clicks at all?

The first three belong to the drafting session, and our [SEO title optimization guide](/en/blog/seo-title-optimization-guide-2026/) covers that half: roughly 580 pixels of desktop width, a band around 40 to 60 characters, and the target phrase placed early. The last two belong to the week after publishing, when the page is live and Google has decided what to display. No preview reaches that far.

Which half you stand in decides which tool is worth opening. For the general field, our roundup of the [best title tag checker tools of 2026](/en/blog/best-title-tag-checker-tools-2026/) compares six free options against one fixed set of criteria. This post is the brand-specific version: you know Moz and want a free equivalent, or a straight answer about what it gives you.

## What does Moz's title preview cover, and where does it stop?

Moz's preview covers the drafting step: paste a draft, see how it reads as a search result snippet, get a length verdict before anything goes live. It stops at the browser window. It does not crawl your published pages, it does not render a phone view, and it never sees click data, because no preview tool can.

Moz pairs that preview with title-tag guidance most of these tools lack: one unique title per page, the brand suffix added only when it earns its space, the main phrase near the front, and title-to-H1 agreement against rewrites. Moz's own guide puts the band suffering the fewest rewrites at 51 to 60 characters.

Two limits deserve naming without turning them into complaints. The preview renders in a desktop browser — Moz's page says so itself — so it does not show what a phone renders. The rest of Moz's workflow, the toolbar, crawl data and rank tracking, sits behind an account and paid tiers whose limits move between releases, so read Moz's current terms, not a number lifted from an older post.

## Which free alternative fits the job you actually have?

Five jobs, five tools, and one tool rarely covers two of them well. Drafting and scoring a new title: a headline analyzer with a mobile preview. Exact truncation verdict: a pixel-counter SERP preview. Competitive context: a SERP simulator. Auditing titles already live: a URL analyzer. Knowing what Google actually published: Search Console URL Inspection.

A drafting tool takes text that has not shipped and judges it. A pixel ruler gives one truncation verdict and nothing else, useful precisely because it refuses to score your copy. A simulator puts your snippet beside the competitors it would sit under. An auditor fetches a published URL and reports what it finds there. Search Console is the only entry reporting what Google chose to show and how often searchers clicked it, which makes it the end of the chain rather than a rival to the others.

## What should you do when two title checkers disagree?

Do not average them. Identify what each tool measured, because checkers that disagree almost always measured different things: one counted characters, one measured pixels, one rendered a phone, one reported what Google chose to publish. Work down that order — characters, then pixels, then Search Console — and the disagreement usually resolves into one verdict.

The common case is a character counter saying the title fits while a pixel simulator says it does not, and both can be right. Fifty-five characters of narrow letters fit where the same count in capitals does not, because Google measures the rendered line. Take the pixel verdict, provided the tool renders Google's font.

The harder disagreement is between a preview and Search Console. Your preview says the title is fine; URL Inspection says Google is serving something else. There is nothing to arbitrate — the preview describes a request, the inspection describes a result. Fix the cause of the rewrite: align the title with the H1, drop repeated phrasing, front-load the phrase, then re-inspect once the change is indexed.

## How do you check a title in five minutes without any tool at all?

Count the characters, read the draft in a window narrowed to phone width, compare the title against the H1, open the live URL in Search Console's URL Inspection, and read the query report. Five steps, no third-party software, and it catches length, intent mismatch, Google's rewrite and the title that actually shipped.

Most title problems are visible without a meter. What a tool adds is speed and a consistent threshold, not information you could not get yourself — so run the manual version once, deliberately, to learn what each automated check is standing in for. If the steps below disagree with each other, you have found the bug before publishing. If they agree and the clicks still do not arrive, the problem is position or intent rather than length. It is also the cheapest version to teach: five minutes, one browser window, no subscription and no licence to renew, so it survives a team change. Hand it to whoever writes the titles, and the check stops being one more thing only you remember to do at all.

1. **Count the characters.** Stay inside the 40 to 60 band and put the target phrase first so truncation leaves it intact.
2. **Read it at phone width.** Narrow the window until lines wrap as on a handset.
3. **Compare title and H1.** Agreement between the two protects you from a Google rewrite.
4. **Run URL Inspection.** Paste the live URL and read the tested HTML — the indexed title.
5. **Read the query report.** Which queries served the page, and did it earn anything?

## Why did Moz-branded queries earn 281 impressions and zero clicks?

Because length was not the cause. Three Moz-related queries in our own Search Console export — "moz title tag checker", "moz title checker" and "moz headline tool" — held 281 impressions between positions 10 and 16 and not one click. A title cut short by a checker would still earn clicks at position 10; these did not.

The obvious reading is the boring one: the page sits on page two, below Moz's own results and the aggregator listicles that already answer the query, so the snippet is rarely seen at all. No title edit repairs a position of 15, and rewriting a title to chase clicks a page-two snippet never received makes the numbers worse. The second reading changes what you build: the query names a brand, and the person typing it already knows Moz. They want an equivalent or an honest account of what it gives them, a narrower question than any generic ranking answers, and that gap is why a brand-specific page beats another list. The ranking is a separate problem no checker here will move.

## Where does Moz fit if you already pay for it?

Keep it. If your team already lives in Moz, leaving the preview where it sits in the workflow is worth more than any marginal difference between free tools. Add the two checks Moz's preview does not run — a pixel-accurate mobile preview before publishing, and Search Console after publishing — and the loop closes.

Continuity has a value no comparison table captures: the person who already has the tab open will actually run the check, and a check that happens beats a better check that does not. Treat free alternatives as additions, not replacements: the preview step stays where your team expects it.

**Before publishing, add** a mobile-width preview from any pixel-accurate tool, so you see the handset rendering instead of assuming the desktop verdict transfers.

**After publishing, add** URL Inspection once the page is indexed, then the performance report after it holds a stable position. That is the only step showing whether the change did anything.

## Which title-checking mistakes cost you the most?

Trusting a pixel number from a tool that does not render Google's font. That one mistake produces confident, wrong verdicts: the tool reports a fit, Google truncates the line, and nothing in the workflow ever looks at the published result, so the error survives every review.

The rest of the list is cheaper to read than to repeat:

- **Checking the draft after publication and never the live URL.** The CMS field and the indexed title drift apart the moment a rewrite intervenes.
- **Optimising a title Google already replaced.** Editing a snippet you are not serving changes nothing until the cause is cleared.
- **Chasing a click gap that is really a position gap.** Below page one impressions are real and exposure is not.
- **Picking a tool by feature count.** Capabilities you never run beat ones you will.
- **Reading two tools as two opinions.** Ask what each measured first.

For the font question specifically: a preview using a generic face and reporting a pixel width is estimating, not measuring. Prefer a tool showing the rendered line and cut point in one view, then confirm against Search Console. The wider on-page work sits in [SEO best practices](/en/blog/seo-best-practices/).
